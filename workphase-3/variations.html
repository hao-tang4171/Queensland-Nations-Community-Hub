4. Design Decisions & Iterations

Each component below records a real design problem, the options considered, and why the final choice was made.

Decision 1 — Glass Card Opacity

The tension

The glass card sits on top of a soft diffuse-light background. If the card is too transparent, the background shows through and reduces text readability. If it is too opaque, it loses the glassmorphism effect that defines the brand.

Iteration

Earlier version

Text on a very translucent card.

rgba(255,252,247, 0.45) — too see-through

Current version

Text on a clearer card.

rgba(255,252,247, 0.72) — readable, still glassy

Outcome

Raised the card background opacity from 0.45 to 0.72. This kept the frosted-glass character while ensuring body text stays legible over the busy background.

Decision 2 — Where to Apply the Diffuse Background

The tension

The diffuse-light gradient background needed to cover the full page. Applying it to body caused a problem: a full-height wrapper (the form wrapper) sat on top of body and covered the background, so the gradient was hidden.

Options considered

Rejected

Apply the background to body — covered by the page wrapper, gradient not visible.

Chosen

Apply the background to html — sits behind everything, so the gradient always shows through regardless of wrappers above it.

Outcome

The diffuse background is defined on the html element. This guarantees a consistent atmosphere across every page without any element accidentally hiding it.

Decision 3 — Audio With Text Transcripts

The tension

Adding audio narration enriches the storytelling and suits a platform about oral cultural heritage. But audio alone excludes users who are deaf or hard of hearing, and is unusable in sound-sensitive settings such as a quiet library or a shared space without headphones.

Options considered

Rejected

Audio only — richest for hearing users, but inaccessible to others.

Rejected

Text only — fully accessible, but loses the warmth and presence of a spoken voice.

Chosen

Audio paired with full written text — every recording is shown alongside its complete text on the same card, so both groups of users are served.

Why this works

Each story's full written text sits on the card next to its audio player, so screen readers can reach it and people can read instead of listen, while hearing users still get the richer audio. Rather than trading accessibility against richness, this serves both — an example of the curb-cut effect, where a feature built for accessibility improves the experience for everyone.

Refinements

  • Audio does not autoplay — avoids startling users and respects quiet settings.
  • preload="none" keeps the page light on mobile data.
  • Each story's written text is shown in full alongside its audio, so the words are always available to read, not hidden behind an extra step.

5. Technical & Structural Decisions

Beyond visual styling, several structural choices shaped how the site behaves and how easily it can grow.

Separating Content from Code

The decision

The cultural narratives are not written directly into the HTML. They live in a single data file (narratives.json), and JavaScript reads that file and builds the cards on the page.

Why

This keeps content and code separate. Adding or editing a story means changing one data file, with no need to touch the HTML or JavaScript. It also means the homepage "Latest Stories" preview and the full Stories page draw from the same source, so they can never fall out of sync.

Subscription as a Modal, Not a Whole Page

The change

In A2 the homepage was essentially a single subscription form. In A3 the homepage became a richer landing page — a hero, community introduction, navigation cards, a story preview, and a cultural-knowledge section — and the subscription form moved into a modal dialog opened by a button.

Why

This lets the homepage do its real job — welcoming people and pointing them into the community — while subscribing becomes an action taken on demand rather than the first and only thing on the page.

How

The modal uses the native <dialog> element, which provides accessibility for free: focus moves into the form on open, the Escape key closes it, and focus returns to the button that opened it. The same dialog pattern is reused on the Events page for the "Join Us" button.

Reusing Components Across Pages

The decision

The glass card, diffuse background, header, footer, and subscription dialog are shared conventions used across every page, each page importing only the components it needs.

Why

Consistency makes the site feel like one place rather than a set of separate pages, and reusing components keeps the code easier to maintain.

6. Cultural Responsibility & Respect

Because this site is about First Nations culture, several decisions were made specifically to treat that culture with care. These choices matter as much as the visual design.

Why We Use Demonstration Content, Not Real Cultural Material

This project does not use real Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander cultural material. This is a deliberate choice for two reasons. First, we have not obtained permission to use such material — and cultural knowledge should only be used with the consent of the communities it belongs to. Second, we have not yet carried out the careful verification that real cultural material would require, and presenting unverified material as authentic would risk spreading misinformation.

Instead, all narratives on this site are fictional and created for demonstration. Where they refer to real institutions (such as AIATSIS or the Queensland Museum) or real principles (such as FPIC and ICIP), each narrative clearly notes what is real and what is invented. This lets the project demonstrate its features while respecting that real cultural material is not ours to use without permission and proper care.

Protecting Knowledge Can Mean Not Publishing It

One narrative deals with a traditional story that belongs to a particular group and is not meant to be shared with outsiders. Rather than writing the story down, it records only that the story exists, who it belongs to, and who is responsible for it — never its content. This reflects an important idea: protecting culture does not always mean making it public. Sometimes the most respectful choice is to preserve something while keeping it within a community.

Asking Before Recording

Another narrative shows a visitor who wants to record a performance but waits, asks the performers for permission, and only records once they agree. This models the principle of consent (FPIC) in an everyday situation, rather than treating cultural expression as something free to capture.

Designing for Everyone

Audio narration is always paired with its full written text, so people who are deaf or hard of hearing, or who simply cannot play sound, are never excluded. The About page opens with an Acknowledgement of Country, and the homepage includes a short section explaining the principles (FPIC, ICIP, and "protect does not always mean publish") that guide how the platform treats cultural material.

7. From A2 to A3

A3 builds directly on the design system established in A2, keeping its foundations while extending its purpose.

What Carried Over

The colour palette (terracotta, ochre, sand), the Quicksand typeface, the glass-card component, the diffuse-light background, and the warm, traditional tone all come from A2 and remain unchanged. The target audience — local residents, community members, and people interested in First Nations culture — is also the same.

What A3 Added

A3 turned a small set of pages into a fuller site: a redesigned homepage with a full-screen hero and a subscription modal; a content submission form; an Events page; an About page; and a new extension — a Stories page that presents audio and visual narratives about protecting culture, driven by a shared data file. The design language stayed the same; what grew was the depth of content and the care taken around cultural responsibility.
